# External names and possible bilingual anchors

Follow-up: the [YBC5447 audit](DATED_DOCUMENT_BRIDGE.md) checks a readable,
dated agreement bearing an ancient Gulf seal impression. Names and goods are
published, but the proposed sealer is inferred from his contract role and no
aligned Indus inscription is authenticated. This is a separate case from the
interpreter's seal and the Meluhha recipient tablets below.

Reviewed 23 September 2026. **No independently verified mapping between an Indus sign sequence and an externally known personal name was identified in this bounded review.** Named contacts, readable cuneiform and Indus-style artifacts are distinct evidence classes. An association with Meluhha does not itself establish a name's language, an Indus spelling, or a seal owner's identity.

## Reviewed candidates

**Subsequent follow-up:** current P453801 access succeeded, and its exact published
handcopy was recovered. It labels the seal unread without drawing seal signs.
The current name reading is Nanasa, superseding the older Nanaza version used in
the initial review below. A possible P516366 duplication and conflicting year
names remain unresolved. See [the follow-up](P453801_FOLLOWUP.md); earlier access
failures here describe the first review, not the current availability.

The [subsequent four-citation audit](MELUHHA_NAME_POOL.md) also resolves
CUSAS40/2,1354 and UCTIS099. The former has a partly readable cuneiform seal legend
with the names lost; the latter is explicitly marked Seal=N in BDTNS. None of
these four citations supplies an established Indus name pairing. The proposed
tests below remain the historical initial plan; the two follow-ups record which
checks have now been completed and which evidence is still missing.

**Shu-ilishu: Louvre AO22310 = CDLI P525331.** The catalogues identify an interpreter of Meluhha; neither supplies an Indus counterpart. Metadata disagree: Louvre assigns Akkad, 2340–2200 BCE and Akkadian; CDLI assigns Ur III and Sumerian. Louvre's Babylonian provenance is attributed from style, not a documented excavation. Preserve these alternatives. [Louvre, inscription and dates/provenance fields](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010147038); [CDLI, Text 1–2 and metadata](https://cdli.earth/artifacts/525331).

Possehl reports checking the inscription with Steve Tinney and making a fresh impression. He also reports unknown findspot and recutting. His suggestion that bilingual tablets might someday emerge is a future possibility, not a claim that this seal is bilingual. The interpreter's occupation alone establishes neither ethnicity nor an Indus phonetic value. [Possehl 2006, pp.42–43, captured web lines37–51](https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/shu-ilishus-cylinder-seal/).

**BM120573 = U.7683.** The museum describes an Indus-style stamp seal bearing a Sumerian inscription, found on the surface at Diqdiqqah, Ur. The [subsequent primary audit](BM120573_CUNEIFORM_AUDIT.md) downloaded Gadd1932 and inspected pp5–6 and PlateI, plus Parpola1994 p131. Gadd's provisional `sak-ku-ši--` has uncertain alternatives and no established meaning; the museum language label is not a solved reading. No corresponding Indus text is present. The original review used indexed text only; that access limitation has now been partly resolved. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1928-1009-56); [Gadd1932](https://ignca.gov.in/Asi_data/33779.pdf).

**Nanaza, Samar and Ali-ahi: Nisaba15,0371 = P453801.** CDLI's historical revision records these persons in oil allocations to people designated Meluhhan (obverse2–5), then an illegible seal entry. This is a specific image-review lead, **not an identified Indus impression or established owner-name match**. The accessible evidence was indexed revision/update text; direct artifact and revision opens failed. Do not silently identify P453801 with another tablet such as P516366. [Revision2249158](https://cdli.earth/inscriptions/2249158); [update event58152](https://cdli.earth/update-events/58152), seal1.

Surdi and De Graef (2025), note22, cite this tablet alongside CUSAS40-2 1354,1582 and UCTIS099, distinguishing foreign names Nanaza/Samar from Akkadian Ali-ahi. Only the indexed note and institutional bibliography were inspected, not the restricted full article or every cited tablet. This supports auditing a linguistically mixed name pool rather than treating it as an Indus dictionary. [Article, note22](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00856401.2025.2488137); [institutional record](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/01JVQPB41XES2ES4GFSBSTW170).

**Older name proposals.** Parpola, Parpola and Brunswig (1977) discuss Sumerian names among Meluhha-associated residents (p.150), propose Ur-du-du for Gadd17 expressly hypothetically (p.159 n.46), and identify Lu-Sunzida in BM86314/CT50,76 as Sumerian while inferring a translated immigrant name (pp.160–161). None supplies an independently established Indus spelling. No glyph IDs were reconstructed from the defective web OCR. [DOI10.2307/3631775](https://doi.org/10.2307/3631775); [author-uploaded text inspected](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274936748_The_Meluhha_Village_Evidence_of_Acculturation_of_Harappan_Traders_in_Late_Third_Millennium_Mesopotamia).

**Pinned Glossa v4.** Sections3.26 and3.28 report no strong match in six names and no discriminative match after expanding to25. These are claimant-reported negative results, not a fresh replication. This review did not independently verify all25 names or execute the matcher. A larger corpus also creates more opportunities for incidental resemblance; it does not solve attribution by itself. [Pinned PDF, page11](../data/raw/glossa_preprint_v4.pdf); [existing methodology review](GLOSSA_METHODOLOGY_REVIEW.md).

## Falsifiable next tests

1. Inspect a primary image/catalogue for **P453801 seal1**, first identifying script and legibility. A readable cuneiform impression would resolve this candidate without an Indus bridge. An Indus sequence would still need independent linkage to a named person or role; recipient and sealer are not automatically identical.
2. Freeze a reading key, direction, segmentation and all phonetic alternatives before predicting whole names on independently attributed impressions. Repeated impressions on separately named documents would be stronger than one resemblance. A wrong prediction on a secure, legible name pairing contradicts that reading.
3. Audit every proposed Meluhhan name to tablet ID, line, date, language and basis of association. Distinguish interpreters, residents, translated names and probable foreign names; unsupported entries cannot act as phonetic anchors.
4. Compare a locked matching rule with unrelated names matched for length and sound structure, including every name, object and reading variant searched. Hold out objects by artifact identity. Better-than-control resemblance still does not establish identity without an external link.

This is not an exhaustive audit of all collections, tablets or modern claims. Parpola's1975 opening extract describes a named cuneiform document bearing an Indus impression as a hoped-for discovery; that historical statement is not proof of universal absence in2026. [Parpola1975, pp.178–209](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0035869X00132873). Failure to find a name here does not disprove writing or personal-name use when owner, language and name-bearing function remain unknown.
