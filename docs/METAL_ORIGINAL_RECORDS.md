# Original Mackay metal-tool records

Mackay's 1938 excavation report directly supplies three present masses, but also says corrosion and chemical separation reduced the objects' weights considerably. These are measurements of surviving, treated objects—not measurements of the complete tools when inscribed. The large axe has an unresolved identifier discrepancy: the primary table prints **DK7535**, whereas Pettersson's later discussion calls it **DK7835**.

## Primary joins and measurements

The secure join is **Plate CXXVI, item number**: printed p454 assigns masses to items2,3,5, and printed p489 tabulates their dimensions, materials, field numbers and context. Images of the original pages were visually inspected; OCR was used only to locate them.

All three table rows give block12A, houseI, room15, level−24.4ft. The ditto marks are expanded in the ledger. Mackay calls item3 little damaged and item5 fairly well preserved; neither phrase establishes the original mass. The262g value used later rounds the primary262.233g. The original also pairs the metric values with imperial quantities; three printed decimal places do **not** establish milligram weighing accuracy.

A close crop reads the axe's imperial quantity as **4lbs3⅛oz**, although the small fraction is a possible printing/scan ambiguity. That reading is inconsistent with1910.030g under modern conversion (about1902.9615g);4lbs3⅜oz would instead be about1910.0489g. This audit preserves the printed metric value rather than repairing the fraction or deriving a replacement mass. The tang is printed5oz13⅓drs, and the small axe9¼oz. Their imperial values also should not be treated as independent milligram measurements.

The7535/7835 disagreement is not resolved as an original misprint, later transcription error or alternate identifier. Plate/item provenance allows the mass comparison to remain explicit without silently correcting either source. This audit has not newly authenticated the incised sign sequences from plate photographs.

## Preservation and reconstruction

Printed p454 says the low-level group had corroded together and was separated in a chemical bath, reducing weight considerably. Mackay explicitly says this prevents accurate testing of the suggested numbers by object weight. No pretreatment total, loss fraction or complete original chisel mass is given in the reviewed passages. This is a bounded negative finding, not a claim that no conservation record exists anywhere.

On printed p473, Mackay classifies CXXVI2 as a type(b) chisel: a flattened, broad rectangular tang narrowing toward a double-sloped edge. Practically only the tang survives. He suggests the tool was originally over15in long; that is a reconstruction, not an intact measurement. Together with the table's5.22in surviving length, this implies a conditional length ratio greater than15/5.22≈2.874. It does **not** establish a mass ratio: the changing cross-section, flattened tang and unknown loss must be accounted for.

No2.6 upper mass factor occurs in these reviewed primary passages. Pettersson's later2.6 factor is a comparative shape reconstruction. Mackay's length estimate neither proves that factor nor supplies a quantitative corrosion allowance. A model using it must remain conditional on those added assumptions.

Mackay also proposes single strokes as digits, invertedU as10, CXXVI5 as76 and CXXVI2 as19. These are the excavator's hypotheses, made in the same paragraph as his caveat about mass testing. His Egyptian comparison supplies no independently known reading of an Indus sign.
