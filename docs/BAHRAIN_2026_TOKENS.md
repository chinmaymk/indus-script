# Bahrain 2026 cuneiform tokens: evidence boundary

Research date: 2026-09-24. **A new publication authenticates a promising cuneiform-name comparandum, but this audit establishes no Indus translation anchor.** The full article, object inventory numbers, complete cuneiform readings and plates were not recovered. All Indus semantic and phonetic values remain unresolved.

Laursen, Al Shaikh, Reeler, Lombard and Marchesi, *Stamped Tokens with Cuneiform Inscriptions from Bahrain: New Light on Kingship and the Gods in Early 2nd Millennium BC Dilmun*, Journal of Near Eastern Studies 85(1), 2026, 115–145, [DOI 10.1086/739820](https://doi.org/10.1086/739820), is a real published article, rather than a proposed work. ## Evidence obtained

The [Bologna author-repository abstract](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/1059790) reports three tokens bearing two seals with cuneiform legends. It attributes both seals to Yaglī-’el and renders his epithet as service to the goddess Panipa. It compares a namesake Dilmun king whose epithet instead concerns Inzak of Agaš, explicitly raising whether these are one person or two. The abstract does not supply its answer, exact sign-by-sign transcription, accession numbers or stratigraphic dates. The repository records a publisher PDF under embargo until **2027-04-01**. Its public HTML is archived as `data/raw/bahrain_2026_unibo.html`.

The [publisher's first-page preview](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/cms/10.1086/739820/asset/a546b601-f5d4-49bb-8a19-4bb1bbf147b9/assets/739820.fp.png) places the discoveries prompting the study at Al Maqsha near Avenue 50 and the Budaiya Highway. It says the Bahrain National Museum had excavated the site since 1978, and refers to tokens 1 and 2. The context sentence continues beyond the page; the missing continuation is not reconstructed. No token or seal photograph appears on this page. The image is archived as `data/raw/bahrain_2026_first_page.png`.

An [announcement by first author Steffen Terp Laursen](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/steffen-terp-laursen-33487267_new-sensational-discovery-royal-seals-activity-7451676890135678976-8qUs), archived separately as `data/raw/bahrain_2026_author_post.html`, reports two Al Maqsha tokens and a third from the Qal’at al-Bahrain palatial quarter bearing a repeated seal. He also reports three additional seals in Style II, associated particularly with Failaka. He identifies the named person with the ruler known from the A’ali tomb and interprets the objects as administrative evidence concerning a king or crown prince. These are the author's public claims; the article's supporting plates and argument were not inspected. His date **1665–1540 BCE concerns the nearby Al Maqsha royal mound**, and must not be silently converted into an independently established date for the tokens.

## Consequences for decipherment

The three objects must not be counted as three independent textual attestations if some impressions repeat a seal. The two cuneiform seals are not two scripts, and different divine epithets do not make the vessel and token legends a bilingual. A repeated personal name alone also cannot resolve whether two historical namesakes are the same individual.

None of the recovered evidence supplies an authenticated Indus sign sequence on these tokens, a shared owner joined to an Indus-inscribed object, or a paired translation. No Mahadevan/Wells identifier can be assigned. The additional Style II impressions are a useful future inspection target: a photograph and exact object identity could permit a motif comparison, but stylistic similarity would still not identify the words of an Indus inscription. The accessible title supplies an early-second-millennium setting; precise chronology requires the unrecovered contexts. No mature-Harappan linguistic continuity is assumed.

This is a bounded finding about the retrieved evidence, **not proof that the complete article contains no Indus comparanda**. The next useful evidence is the complete publication with its catalogue, inscriptions and plates, followed by any exact, independently authenticated Indus object join. Search-result paraphrases and Wikipedia's crown-prince summary cannot substitute for that work.
