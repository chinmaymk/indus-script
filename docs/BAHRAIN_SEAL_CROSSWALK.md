# Bahrain seal identities and the missing alignment

The older three-name tablet and the newly published 2026 tokens are separate cases. Neither recovered evidence set establishes a pairing between a cuneiform name and a particular Indus inscription.

Laursen's [2010 primary article](https://www.harappa.com/sites/default/files/pdf/The_westward_transmission_of_Indus_Valle.pdf), Table1 on printed100/PDF5, supplies this limited catalogue crosswalk. It was read through web extraction; the complete PDF could not be downloaded and its illustrations were not visually verified in this phase.

The two occurrences of **279 belong to different catalogues and different objects**. No Mahadevan identifier or sign value follows from them. The current evidence does not establish which Qala’at seal was nearest the three-name tablet. Published local catalogue numbers are not museum accession numbers.

The chronology discussion on printed121–122 places seven Gulf Type seals in periodIIa, about2050–2000BCE, with one possiblyIb: two inscribed, five uninscribed. This is a phase-level association. It does not identify a seal owner, a name shared by two inscriptions, or an impression of one of these seals on the tablet. The paper's statement on printed104 that associated impressions had not been found is a historical statement about its evidence, not a claim about every subsequent discovery.

Parpola1994 printed11/PDF29 was checked visually against the retained original scan. It reports a Bahrain Indus seal associated with a tablet containing three Amorite names and a palaeographic date around2050–1900BCE. Its adjacent Fig1.7 is **Lothal L-123**, not the Bahrain seal. Printed130/PDF148 repeats the Bahrain association while separately discussing the Yale mercantile contract; neither passage licenses joining those objects. The primary scan and selected extraction are pinned in `data/processed/bahrain_parent_review.json`.

For a name hypothesis, the decisive missing evidence is an independently demonstrated join: an identifiable impression on a named document, a shared owner established outside the proposed reading, or a bilingual inscription. Three people named on a nearby tablet leave several candidate referents; an Indus seal could also label an institution, office, transaction or something absent from the tablet. A father–son relationship read in cuneiform therefore supplies no Indus kinship sign by itself. These alternatives are logical possibilities, not equally probable historical conclusions.
