Inscriptions in Southern Vietnam and Southeast Asia during the 1 st millennium CE are mainly originated 
from Indian alphabet, the Brahmi in particular. However, the questions of when and how Brahmi alphabet 
was transferred and spread among the societies in Southern Vietnam are still in obscure.
This paper presents result of collecting archaeological remains incised with inscriptions. They are mainly 
gold plaques deposited under the foundation of Buddhist and Hindu architectures and some other types 
of archaeological artefacts. Very few of them are deciphered due to the lack of expertise on this field 
in Vietnam. Therefore, the dissemination of this data source aims at seeking expertise on the study of 
paleography and language of ancient people in Southern Vietnam. They will also a good source for the 
study of cultural interaction between Southeast Asian countries and Indian civilization.
Keywords: Brahmi, inscription, paleography, Southern Vietnam, Oc Eo culture
Introduction
In late 19 th - early 20 th centuries, among the artefacts collected by the French, many stone slabs 
carved with old Indian scripts have been discovered. From the 1940s, Louis Malleret collected a large 
number of tiny artefacts, including the ornaments, pendants, seals and sealing. Many of them were incised 
with short inscriptions. In the late 20th century and early decade of the 21st century, a large number of artefacts 
bearing short inscriptions, signs or symbols have been unearthed from the archaeological excavations. They 
include the terracotta seals, short inscriptions on stone moulds or potsherds. Particularly, the gold plagues 
found from the temple consecrated deposits provide a rich source of data for the study of not only religious 
ceremony, iconography but also paleography. The most essential collections of artefacts related to the 
writing are found from Go Thap (Dong Thap province), Da Noi (An Giang province), Go Xoai (Long An 
province) and Cat Tien (Lam Dong province) (Le 2005a). In addition, some small artefacts found from Oc 
Eo port-city (An Giang province) and Nhon Thanh (Can Tho province) also provide important evidence 
of inscription used in daily life of the people. Except the inscription from Go Xoai site, which bear the 
Buddhist mantra “Ye, Dhama…”, other artefacts mainly have very short inscription. The most typical sites 
will be introduced in this paper (Figure 30.1).
Go Thap site (or Prasat Pram Loven) is located in hamlet I, Tan Kieu commune, Thap Muoi district, 
Dong Thap province. The site is well known, thanks to the discovery of many architectural ruins, remains 
of settlements areas, cemetery and a large number of artefacts, including the stone and wood sculptures of 
Hindu gods and Buddha images. Eight stone stelae found from this site are dated from the 5
th to the 7 th- 
8th centuries CE. The earliest one (K5) refers to the name Gurnavarman, the prince of Kaundinya family 
(Malleret 1963; Coedés 1931, 6-7, Le et al. 1995; Le 2005b). Among the most important discoveries in 
this site, the gold plaques found in situ as temple consecrated deposit are noteworthy not only for their 
iconographical features, but also the short inscriptions that preserve the most archaic forms found so far 
from southern Vietnam.

The Oc Eo site spreads in a large area of about 15 km in each side of An Giang and Kien Giang 
provinces, including the Oc Eo rice field and several scattered granite mountains with the highest one being 
Mount Ba The (Mallerret 1959, 75-24). The site is well known as a port-city of the Funan Empire. Several 
satellite sites might have a relationship with the city, including the Giong Xoai site. This is a large sand 
dune located 1km to the south of Mount Ba The. From this site, Buddhist and Hindu sculptures, and a huge 
architectural foundation containing gold plaques were found in 2001.
Da Noi is a notable site in Long Xuyen (An Giang province). Oc Eo typed pottery, many wood stilts 
and large sized wood columns have been found scattered in the field, in between the mounds and along the 
ancient canals (Le and Witzel 2012). In 1985, many gold plaques incised with images were unearthed from 
the architectural foundations distributed in an area of 150 ha. This is invaluable evidence for the study of 
Hindu iconography and inscription during the heyday of the Oc Eo culture in 4
th - 6th centuries CE (Le et al.  
1995, 30; Dao 1995). 
Cat Tien site is located in Quang Ngai commune, Cat Tien district, Lam Dong province. In 1984-
1985, remains of several architectures were discovered. Between 1994-1997 and 2001-2003, the ruins of 
12 architectural foundations were unearthed by the large-scaled excavations conducted by the Institute of 
Archaeology (Hanoi) and the Center for Archaeology (Ho Chi Minh City). They are identified as Hindu 
temples, based on architectural features; worshiping sculptures, such as the Sivalinga, images of the goddess 
Mahishamardini and Ganesa. Particularly, the temple consecrated deposits, including many gold plaques 
with short inscription, were found in situ from the central sanctum room under the main foundation of these 
temples. In addition, other types of archaeological remains were also unearthed, such as settlement areas, 
vestiges of road and lake, and grave sites. Several scientific reports and research papers highlight the unique 
value of archaeological data of the Cat Tien site (Nguyen 2002; 2005; Le Dinh Phung 2006; Witzel and  
Le 2007; Le 2011, Le and Witzel 2012).
There are many gold plaques incised with inscriptions or signs found from the mentioned sites, but 
not many are clearly recognized and deciphered due to the lack of expertise in this field. The author of this 
paper, although not a specialist in Brahmi paleography, spent almost 20 years in collecting data related to 
this topic. During 2006-2007, in co-ordination with Prof. Michel Witzel (Harvard University), the analyses 
of the inscriptions from the photograph and eye copy have been made. A number of the inscriptions are 
transcribed and some of them are initially interpreted by him. Samples with typical scripts selected and 
reported in this paper are for encouraging further study on this data sources and seeking for special expertise 
in language and ancient paleography.
Inscriptions found from Go Thap site 
Except the mentioned stone inscriptions, several short inscriptions have been recognized on the 
potsherds and gold plaques. From Go Thap site, at least two potsherds incised with Brahmi inscription have 
been found. The first potsherd is a very fine one found from a test pit in 2009. Its surface is in buff color, the 
body and the inner surface is grey. Five letters of the Pallava style can be recognized, which have been read 
as “ba su taa la kii ” (Figure 30.2) by Anna Slaczka and William Southworth (personal communication in 
2012). The second potsherd was unearthed during the construction of the protecting roof of the architectural 
remains at the Minh Su mound in 2012 (Figure 30.3). It is also of fine pottery but contained some small 
organic inclusion. The outer surface is in reddish color, the inner, whitish grey. It is difficult to define the 
exact number of the letters. Seven letters are recognizable in the first line and probably two letters in the 
second line. It has not yet been transliterated.
More than 300 gold plaques have been unearthed from the central brick blocks of seven architectural 
foundations at Go Mo (Cemetery Mound) located in the centre of the protected area (Dao 1995; Le 2005a). 
Many of them are incised with the symbols and incarnations of gods and goddesses. There are 38 pieces 
which were incised with inscription or signs. Many letters have not yet been identified clearly due to very 
blurred and disturbed lines engraved on small and thin gold foils. Very few inscriptions can be understood 
thoroughly. However, their archaic forms are noteworthy. Some typical examples are discussed below.

Inscription found from Da Noi site
From the foundation of several architectural remains at Da Noi site, An Giang province, a large 
number of gold artefacts have been discovered. From the photographs of these artefacts, inscriptions and 
signs or symbols have been recognized on 27 samples. 
In 2001, 17 samples from the architectural foundation DN.M3 were interpreted (Witzel & Le 
2001). On basis of paleographical features, it is suggested that they represent the influence of the Pallava 
script and datable to the 4th-6th century CE. Most of them are very short and represent the name of the image 
appearing on the gold plaque, such as Vajra, Padma, Tri’ sula, Sesa. Some inscriptions are longer with more 
complicated content, such as Varahasghami, Dana’ sa…sra; Vraddhibha dhvaja etc. The images were also 
depicted in more advanced style, in comparison with those from the Go Thap site (Le 2006, 136, H. 224).
Ten other pieces from the architectural foundations DN.M2 and DN.M4 represent the same 
paleographical features, with no or not very clear images. The meanings have not yet been fully deciphered.

Inscription found from Cat Tien site 
Some inscriptions on the gold plaques found from Cat Tien have been studied in more detail (Witzel 
and Le, 2007; Le and Witzel 2012). However, further study is necessary, particularly in terms of language 
and meaning. 
The consecrated deposit of the temple G1A
From the foundation of temple G1A, located on the biggest mound of the Cat Tien site complex, a 
large number of artefacts, mainly gold plaques, were unearthed. 43 pieces of gold plaques are incised with 
inscriptions and signs or letters. The longest inscriptions include 10 to15 syllables. Most of them include 
from two to five syllables, or even only one syllable. It is noticeable that the letters were not always clearly 
and properly written. The analysis of the paleography shows that the word Pu nya(m) (meaning auspicious 
or merit) appears at least 13 times. The prefix ku and/or ka appear 12 times. This prefix might have denoted 
male or female and probably followed by a personal name. On nine gold plaques, the inscription appears 
together with a drawing and it probably represented the same meaning. The meaning of several inscriptions 
has not yet been understood thoroughly. However, the appearance of the word Pu nya(m)and the prefix ku 
and/or ka indicates that these are the prayers or offerings to the Hindu gods and goddesses. Their personal 
names are also appeared (Le and Witzel 2012).
Paleographical features of the inscriptions on several gold plaques were mainly influenced from 
the Pallava inscriptions as those from the Da Noi site. Local ways of pronunciation of several syllables are 
recognized. However, the representation of the Hindu-pantheon seems to be the result of influence from a 
newly developed toward Indian Hinduism, which most probably flourished during the 6th-8th centuries CE.

The consecrated deposit of the temple G6B
From the foundation of the temple G6B located on the same mound Go 6, a group of artefacts 
has been discovered. From several photographs provided by the Lam Dong Provincial Museum, 10 are 
incised with short inscriptions in late Brahmi and deciphered by Prof. M. Witzel in 2007. The names of the 
Dikpalakas appeared on eight pieces, whereas, the name of the Brahma and the Vedi appeared on other two 
(Witzel and Le 2007; Le 2011, 422). It is noteworthy that several letters represent similar style with that 
of the Kadamba inscription of the Eastern Calukyas and the inscription of the Pallavas during the 5 th-7th 
centuries CE (Bühler 1896, Tab. VII: 31, 35/XIII-XXIII). Some words, for example the “Vedi”, are very 
close in style with the Pallava inscriptions of Dharmaraja and Kailasan of the 7thcentury(Bühler 1896, Tab. 
VII: 26/XXIII, 40/XXII, 46/XXIII).
Thus, we found the names of all the gods who reside in the four directions and four intermediate 
directions, as well as the name of Brahma, who resides in the center of a mandala - the sacred enclosure or 
the temple itself. Paleographical features show that the inscriptions are well written in late Brahmi and in 
close style with the Southern inscriptions of the Chalukya and Pallava, especially those of Kadambar during 
the 5th-7th centuries CE. However, in several cases, the changes in syllable or the curious way of writing 
indicate a local style of reading and writing Sanskrit words. From paleographical background, it seems that 
the temple G6B in Cat Tien was built after the 7
th century CE.
Summation
The archaeological data unearthed from the mentioned sites in Southern Vietnam provide important 
source for the study of not only architecture, art and religion but also the history of propagation of Brahmi 
and Sanskrit into Southern Vietnam. 
From a paleographic perspective, several inscriptions on gold plaques from Go Thap represent very 
archaic types of Brahmi which show the similarity with the inscriptions in various areas of ancient India. 
Both the northern and southern Indian styles of scripts seem to be known by the people, particularly that 
of the Kushana-Satavahana period. The contacts with the Northeast and Southern India in early period, 
roughly from the last centuries BC to about the 4
th-5th centuries AD, can also be reconfirmed by the nature 
of religious symbols, pottery and Buddha images in wood etc. (Le 2006, 42-44; 2011). 
During the heyday of the Oc Eo culture (4 th-6th centuries CE), the presence of the scripts of south 
Indian styles, the Pallava in particular, are noteworthy from Go Thap, Giong Xoai and Da Noi sites. In 
addition to the gold plaques, short inscriptions are found also on pottery, mainly the fine bodied vases or 
jars from Go Thap site and on moulds for manufacturing metal ornaments from Nhon Thanh site (Can Tho 
province). During this period, the best works of art representing the influences of Gupta and late Gupta 
art styles were presented in many sites in the low plain of Southern Vietnam (Le 2006, 44-53, 63-66). The 
comparative study of inscriptions, iconography and art styles will be useful to understand more clearly the 
nature of contacts and cultural interaction between the low plain of Southern Vietnam and the outside world 
during this period.

Inscriptions found from the G1A temple in Cat Tien site still show a strong influence of the Pallava 
inscription during the 4 th - 6 th centuries CE (Le 2006, 143). It is difficult to identify the style of the inscriptions 
found from temple G2A, although elements of eastern and southern Indian scripts are observable. Although 
preserving the archaic type of Kushana inscriptions, artistic features on the gold plaques found from the 
G6A temple indicate a later date. The inscriptions found from the G6B temple are well written in Brahmi 
and in close style with the Southern inscriptions of the Chalukya and Pallava, especially those of Kadambar 
during the 5
th-7 th centuries CE. However, in several cases, the changes in syllable or curious way of writing 
indicate a local way of reading and writing the Sanskrit words. The inscriptions of the temple G3 in Cat 
Tien site may represent the latest elements of the scripts in the discussed sites, although a typical style of 
this group is not yet identifiable. In addition to the long time bracket of the chronology and mainly southern 
Indian styles of the scripts, the Cat Tien site represents development in Hindu iconography, with a rich 
pantheon occupied by various human forms of gods and goddesses. They indicate the adoption of a new 
tendency in Hindu art of India which probably flourished during the 6
th-7th centuries CE, and provided 
religious inspiration to the Cat Tien people in the late centuries of the first millennium CE (Le 2011). 
The answers for several issues related to the languages and the people of the mentioned sites are 
still vague. The interpretation of these inscriptions and the images are still far from completeness. Further 
efforts from experts in this field may bring more useful information for the study of not only Southeast 
Asian but also Indian history of cultural contacts and evolution.
