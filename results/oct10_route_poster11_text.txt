Since 2016, the extensive excavation at Mound II, Chanhu-daro, has
made it possible to document, for the very first time in the lower
Indus Valley and over a very large area (6,500 m²), a planned city
built of mud brick dated from the first centuries of the Indus Civiliz a-
tion (2600/2500-2300 BCE). Another major result of the 2015-2022
campaigns is the discovery, in most of the excavated neighborhoods,
of craft workshops specialized in lapidary working. These workshops
yielded tens of thousands of contextualized remains associated with
bead and ornament manufacturing (steatite, agate,carnelian, jasper
and other semi-precious stones as lapis-lazuli). The ongoing study of
lithics, conducted by Dr. Florine Marchand (University of Liège, Bel-
gium), allows to accurately reconstruct technologies used by Indus
craftsmen.
The craft remains illustrate the entire process of stone bead manufa c-
turing, from knapping/cutting, and preforming the various materials
to perforating and polishing the objects. They also provide evidence
of tool manufacturing (chert blades and bladelets, knives, burins, er-
nestite drills, and macro-tools such as grinders, hammers and polish-
ers).
This collection is unique for the entire Greater Indus Valley. Spatial
analysis of the remains indicates that the workshops were located in
the very heart of the city; they were “multi-material” and sectorized
according to the different stages of the crafting stages.
As a major center of production since the beginning of the Indus Ci v-
ilization, the city may have been entirely devoted to the lapidaries
working. Although the scale and intensity of production remain diff i-
cult to determine, the current data suggest that Chanhu-daro may
have been a “workshop city” in which most aspects of daily life could
have been organized around craftsmanship.
The excavations also provided insight into the urban layout during 
the first Indus period (2600/2500-2300 BCE). The urban fabric was 
very dense and subdivided into several neighborhoods or blocks, 
demonstrating a certain homogeneity and stability over time. Each 
block included at least a dozen of adjoining mud-brick houses, quite 
modest in size. These houses had a tripartite layout with a 
quadrangular or L-shaped courtyard dedicated to domestic activities
(cooking and storage areas) and craft activities. The courtyards were 
flanked by rooms and small cells of various sizes. Some houses had 
buttresses, facade elements, or storage compartments made of fired 
brick. A very few of them had small bathing pavements.
The houses have all yielded very large quantities of archaeological 
artifacts. Hundreds of complete ceramic vessels, hundreds of 
thousands of potsherds, and more than 80,000 objects have been 
discovered there to date, including terracotta figurines, bangles, 
beads, balls, rattles, minia ture carts, weaving tools, but also stone 
beads, shell bangles and a few ste atite seals, stone cubic weights and 
copper-metal objects.
