Abstract
Indus inscriptions hold the key to unlocking the history of pre-Iron Age India and
all Indo-European peoples but remain undeciphered for over a century. All prior
attempts have been partial, unsatisfactory and unfalsifiable. We decipher the In-
dus script by treating it as a large cryptogram as described by Claude Shannon.
We decipher every sign sequentially using regular expressions and set-intersection.
Indus script is discovered to be proto-abugida segmental with signs for consonants
and vowels. Indus inscriptions are in grammatically correct post-Vedic Sanskrit.
Variants of 76 allographs constitute most signs. Conjunct signs constitute the rest.
Our decipherment can read every inscription and we translate 500+ inscriptions in-
cluding the 50+ longest, 50+ shortest and 400+ medium-sized inscriptions including
100+ inscriptions with conjunct signs. We comfortably surpass Shannon’s criteria
for a credible cryptogram decipherment. Brahmi glyphs are discovered to be stan-
dardized Indus signs. We find significant continuation of Indus linguistic features
and cultural elements in post-bronze age India.

Indus Valley Civilization; Indus script; Epigraphy; Brahmi; Sanskrit
1. Introduction1
Indus Valley civilization was the largest bronze age civilization, spanning over one mil-2
lion square kilometers and having an estimated population of five million (
Dixit, 2019).3
This advanced civilization featured planned cities, drainage, international trade and4
standardized weights among many impressive accomplishments. Indus civilization has5
left behind strings of symbols on seals, tablets and a large sign (Dholavira signboard)6
which is termed “the Indus script.” Although the mature Indus civilization is believed7
to have started around 2600 BCE, we see stage-by-stage evolution from the earliest8
sites to Bhirrana and Mehrgarh 7000 BCE. These older stages are given different names9
such as pre-Harappan, early-Harappan and so on to distinguish them from the mature10
Harappan stage.11

1.2. The corpus30
There are 4300+ inscriptions from archaeological digs ( Wells and Fuls, 2023). More31
are being unearthed as new sites are excavated. The stratigraphy, location and other32
details of most later excavations are preserved but many older finds have missing data.33
The median inscription length is about 5 signs. There are about 50 inscriptions that34
are 10 signs or longer. Some inscriptions are a single sign or two signs. The seals35
themselves are small and come in all manner of shapes: square, rectangular, circular36
and triangular. The square seals are sized about 3 ×3 to 4 ×4 cm. Due to the scarce37
space, we see the crowding of letters to the left side in early inscriptions. The script38
has a large variety of signs that are hand carved and many signs show abstraction,39
simplification and rotation, evidently to preserve scarce horizontal space. The oldest40
layer of Harappa shows only about 71 signs, possibly indicating that some later signs41
are variants (Konasukawa, 2020).42
In addition to seals, we have tablets and tags, that were presumably used to attach43
to goods for trade. These show heavy wear similar to old coins and are found close to44
market areas, which may be taken as evidence of use as currency. Many seals indeed45
resemble Gupta seals and early Janapada coins with an inscription on top, an animal46
icon in the center and sometimes a staff or standard in front of the animal (Srivatsava,47
2021).48
The Dholavira signboard is meant to be seen from a large distance to convey informa-49
tion to travelers and may be evidence that the Indus script is a form of writing. Writing50

indicate that IVC was a literate society. Indus characters have also been found carved52

2. Methods60
2.1. Script Analysis61
The oldest evidence of the Indus script is graffiti on potsherds from 4000 BCE Balakot.62
There are both abstracted and elaborate versions of signs in this phase. Whether the63
script originally began as a pre-writing system similar to cuneiform cannot be deter-64
mined using artifacts from this phase. The script seems to have evolved into a uniform65
writing system over a millennium. By the mature phase ~2600 BCE, inscriptions are66
fairly consistent over time and space.67
2.1.1. Script direction68
The preferred script direction seems to be right-to-left as evident from external and69
internal evidence (Mahadevan, 1977). External evidence such as crowding on the left70
side on inscribed copper objects, bas-relief tablets, seal impressions (right side on seals),71
evidence of starting carving letters from the right side and so on was combined with72
internal evidence such as identical inscriptions appearing as both one and two line73
versions. This enabled the eventual determination of the direction of most inscriptions.74
Approximately 83% are determined to be right-to-left and 6.5% are left-to-right. The75
rest are either single-sign, undetermined or damaged. A mathematical model of the gini-76
coefficient of the Indus corpus also finds that the Indus script is written right-to-left77
regardless of the type of script (Ashraf and Sinha, 2018).78
2.1.2. The signary79
Mahadevan classifies the Indus script into 417 signs, ICIT classifies them into 700+ signs80
(Wells and Fuls, 2023), treating minor variants as separate signs since a sizable number81
(1000+) of signs is suitable for a logosyllabic analysis. On the other extreme, S. R.82
Rao claimed that the script only had 20 or so signs (Rao, 1980). The obvious problem83
with a low sign count is that the number of legal words formed by adjacent signs drops84
precipitously. There will be many instances of repeated syllables such as kakajakaja,85
making reading anything but the shortest inscriptions impossible. Rao was forced to86
claim that the Indus Valley language was monosyllabic. Essentially he acknowledged87
that he could not find even a small number of legal words formed by the interlocking88
of his assigned values. Maximizing the signary size increases the possible matches of89
legal words. For example, if we have a partially deciphered stringπAT , we may choose90
to assign C to π to read CAT . However, if there is another sequence ΠπT , we get91
a higher readability by choosing A for Π to read ACT , even though the signs π and92
Π look similar. Logosyllabic analysis also benefits from a large signary by claiming93
related meanings for similar signs, such as Parpola’s star  vs planet  for variants of94
the fish sign. This is a workaround to avoid nonsensical logosyllabic readings such as95
‘star-star-jar’, which may now be read slightly better as ‘star-Saturn-jar.’96
However, a large signary is untenable on thorough analysis. Inscriptions up to Harap-97
pan period 3A show only 71 signs ( Konasukawa, 2020). It is improbable for a large98
signary script to exist from 4000 BCE to 2600 BCE with only 71 signs attested. On99
examination, we find that many allegedly different signs that are attested post the100
Harappan period 3A are cursive forms     , simplified abstractions      ,101
embellished   and stylistic variants    or strings of pre-existing signs . Sim-102
plified forms of complex signs are normal in long-lived scripts when the medium is not103
conducive to cursive development, for example as in Sumerian cuneiform. Cursive forms104

2.1.3. Script Type112
Logographic scripts are characterized by low frequency and a large signary. Only a small113
fraction of the total 417 Indus script signs (27.5%) are attested 10 or more times and114
only a tiny fraction (6%) have an attestation count of 100+. A significant fraction of115
the rare signs seem to be stylistic variants, accidentally mirrored signs, cursive forms or116
word fragments. Gradual abstractions of pictorial signs such as   must be seen117

2.2. Cryptanalytic Decipherments145
Some scripts such as the Copiale cipher have been deciphered using cryptanalysis146
(Knight, Megyesi, and Schaefer, 2011). The corpus of inscriptions in an unknown script147
represents the ciphertext and the source language represents the plaintext. The key148
is the assignment of the script signs to its values. The output of the cryptanalysis of149
the script is its decipherment. This model has worked several times in history. Ephron150
was able to re-decipher the Ventris-Chadwick decipherment of Linear-B using crypt-151
analysis (Ephron, 1961). He only found one sign that had a different value, which was152
later acknowledged as correct by the original decipherers, showing the superiority of153
cryptanalytic decipherments.154
2.3. Cryptograms155
A syllabic or phonetic script can be modeled as a cipher and solved using proven math-156
ematical methods. A cryptogram simply stated, is a message in a known language157
encoded in an unknown script. A specific kind of cipher that has been thoroughly158
studied is the single substitution cipher or cryptogram, where one sign in the script159
corresponds to a single phoneme, syllable or alphabet. Newspapers and puzzles often160
carry such cryptograms on their puzzle page. Typically the encoded message is a quote161
by a famous person and is long enough to be uniquely deciphered. A homophonic cipher162
is a variant of a cryptogram that assigns multiple signs per phoneme and may be used163
to model scripts with allographs.164
All available ciphertext for a given key constitutes a cryptogram regardless if the en-165
cipherer sent the ciphertext in different pieces or logical units or at different times. This166
may be trivially verified by picking random words from various websites, feeding them167
into a cryptogram creator and solving on a cryptogram solver such as quipqiup.com.168
This is because the cipher is a transformation function and all input to it is the169
plaintext and all output is the cryptogram:170
If M is the message, K the key, and E the enciphered message, or cryptogram, we have171
E = f (M, K ). In other words, E is a function of M and K. We prefer to think of this,172
however, not as a function of two variables but as a (one parameter) family of operations173
or transformations, and we write it E = TiM The transformation Ti applied to message174
M produces cryptogram E (Shannon, 1945).175
There are two famous historical examples where separately captured messages have176
been pooled together and used to break ciphers. First, the cipher of Mary, Queen177
of Scots was cracked by treating 50+ of her enciphered letters as a single cryptogram178
(George Lasry and Tomokiyo, 2023). Second, the Zodiac-340 cipher was mailed to three179
separate newspapers and was cracked by analyzing them as a single cryptogram.180
The captured ciphertext doesn’t need to be contiguous or even the same logical unit181
of message, because every word of ciphertext reduces the equivocation of the key by182
increasing the information we have about the legal adjacencies that may appear in the183
plaintext. Language is a Markov process and the words and sentences in a language184
form a graph representing the output of the process. These are termed “residue classes”185
by Shannon. Residue classes are a subgraph of the Markov process. Possession of such186
a subgraph reduces the equivocation dramatically. The more residue classes that have187
been intercepted, the larger the subgraph and the smaller the equivocation. On average,188
1/ρ residue classes of short words will enable the recovery of at least one sign value,189
where ρ is the redundancy of the language. For most natural languages with ρ ≈ 0. 7,190

2.4. Shannon on Cryptograms200
Claude Shannon’s groundbreaking A Mathematical Theory of Cryptography is a foun-201
dational work of modern cryptography, signaling, data compression and information202
theory (Shannon, 1945). This work addresses the mathematics and cryptanalysis of203
cryptograms. According to Shannon, an unknown script such as the Bacon/Voynich204
Manuscript may be modeled as a cryptogram (Bacon, 1401-1599). Our method to deci-205
pher the Indus script essentially reuses Shannon’s cryptanalysis methods faithfully with206
the help of modern programming languages.207
A cryptogram may have many possible solutions, for example: the encrypted text208
αβγ has ≈556 solutions, since any 3-letter word with no repeating letters is a solution.209
Others like ǫǫλ only have a unique solution: EEL.210
The question of the uniqueness of solutions to ciphers in general (as opposed to the211
hand-crafted examples above) is useful to determine whether a decipherment is valid.212
Shannon’s criteria for a cipher having a unique solution is determined by a quantity213
termed “equivocation.”214
2.4.1. Redundancy, Entropy and Equivocation215
All natural languages have information content that is represented by symbols. Consider216
the simple yes-or-no question: “Are you over the age of 18?” The answer to this question217
may be represented as either of the words YES or NO. One does not need 5 total218
symbols to denote this information, Y, N or even 1, 0 are sufficient. There are exactly219
two alternative answers to this question and therefore it represents log22 = 1 bit of220
information. The answer to whether the person is over the age of 18 therefore, can be221
encoded as one binary digit. Similarly, the information regarding the day of the week222
requires log27 ≈ 2. 8 bits.223
Natural languages use words and not bits and therefore use significantly more symbols224
than necessary to transmit the same amount of information. Natural languages need to225
use excess symbols because of dependencies and rules on how symbols are put together226
to form words and sentences. This means that in natural languages, unlike optimized227
binary notation, many combinations of phonemes (or letters or syllables) are illegal228
words and are meaningless. For example, for the day of the week, if the first letter is229
known to be T, then the next letter can only be {u, h} (for Tuesday and Thursday).230
Other letters such as x, f are invalid as would give Tx, Tf. This inefficiency and excess231
use of symbols is redundancy.232
When we decipher a symbol or make a choice of value, we gain information about233
the plaintext. The quantity of information gained when we make a choice is entropy.234
Its counterpart, the available meaningful choices we can make about the unknown is235
equivocation.236

2.4.2. Unicity distance244
The key space is the number of possible keys in a cipher. For a single substitution cipher,245
the key space is the number of ways to rearrange the plaintext alphabet to create the246
cryptogram alphabet. For an English single substitution cipher, the number of keys is247
26 × 25 × ... 2 × 1 = 26! The information contained in the key space is log2(26!) ≈ 88. 4248
bits. A decipherment that reads much less than the information size of the key space is249
dubious because the information of the deciphered text may exist in the equivocation of250
the key space itself and may not be connected in any way to the ciphertext. A reliable251
decipherment, therefore, must decipher plaintext of a length beyond the information252
size of the key space. After a certain amount of plaintext is deciphered, equivocation253
becomes zero and the cryptogram has a unique solution. This length is called theunicity254
distance. The reliability of a decipherment that reads beyond the unicity distance may255
be explained as the impossibility of information being created ex nihilo . If meaningful256
information larger than the key space is extracted, then it must certainly be from the257
ciphertext and not from the key.258
A source language represents the plaintext, consisting ofp symbols, each representing259
a letter, syllable or phoneme. A homophonic cipher of N symbols, N > p , may be260
constructed by mapping each ciphertext symbol to a plaintext symbol. Each ciphertext261
symbol may be assigned to a plaintext symbol in p ways, thus carrying log2p bits of262
information per symbol.263
The number of possible keys in a homophonic cipher with N ciphertext symbols264
mapped to p plaintext symbols is < p N. Its key space is |K| ≈ log2pN bits. The265
redundancy of the plaintext language is denoted by ρ which is ≈ 0. 7 for most natural266
languages.267
A large key space causes a large equivocation resulting in many possible meaningful268
but false decipherments. The equivocation is also inversely proportional to the redun-269
dancy of the language. Languages with low redundancy will have many more spurious270
matches compared to languages with high redundancy. The length of the deciphered271
plaintext that equals the information content of the key is known as the unicity distance272
and is given by:273

2.5. Previous decipherments291
It is impractical to enumerate and falsify the 100+ attempted decipherments of the292
Indus script. Most are not serious attempts, but simply insightful observations and293
preliminary attempts to read a dozen or so signs and as many inscriptions. These are294
dubious for the same methodological flaws in the somewhat serious attempts that read295
larger amounts of text as described below.296
2.5.1. Logographic and Logosyllabic297
Most attempted decipherments of Indus script are logographic or logosyllabic, avoiding298
the need for syllables or phonemes to interlock into words. A majority of signs can be299
claimed to be single words, most inscriptions are read as nouns with occasional generic300
verbs. This enables short inscriptions to be read as adjective-noun or verb-adjective-301
noun. The method starts by assuming the value of a sign and then tries to read a302
meaning into a short inscription.303
Parpola for example, considers the intersecting circles sign  to represent bangles304
and three strokes sign  to represent hearth (Parpola, 1994). The reasoning for such305
an assumption is unexplained. These two signs may as well represent nuts and three or306
wheels and baby teeth or any thousands of competing concepts. The unicity distance307
of such decipherments is at least the entire corpus. Occasionally, these signs occur in308
meaningless patterns and need an alternate explanation. For example, when these two309
signs are combined , the reading hearth bangles would be meaningless, so a new310
overloaded interpretation is added: pregnancy bangles.311
Incrementing the key space for every permutation of signs causes the key space to be312
always larger than the entire corpus. Therefore, all decipherments using this method313
are dubious. The information content of the readings is from the key and not from the314
ciphertext. This is why there exist dozens of logographic and rebus decipherments that315
read a lot of text but in ways completely unrelated to each other. A Rosetta stone316
would make a logographic decipherment credible since it would double the information317
content read and go beyond the unicity distance.318
In some decipherments, some sign values are borrowed from other scripts. Signs are319
assumed to have the same value as any similar sign in other scripts. Mahadevan for320
example, assumes symbols similar to Egyptian hieroglyphs and Sumerian cuneiform321

2.5.2. Borrowed starter set340
Eclectic collation of sign values from many different scripts is the central method of341
some syllabic and phonetic decipherments. Their advantage over logographic claims is342
that they can use imported sign values as a starter set and discover the values of the343
remainder using dictionary search and guesswork. S. R. Rao pioneered this technique344
by claiming that similar-looking Semitic and Indus signs have the same values ( Rao,345
1980). He used the Semitic starter set supported simply by his assumption that Indus346
signs eventually developed into Semitic signs.347
The defect in this method is that the choice of a starter set is fairly arbitrary. Any two348
scripts will have some signs that look similar or similar enough with completely different349
sound values. Roughly 33% of both Linear-B and Caroline Island appear similar to350
signs from Indus script but Indus inscriptions are unreadable if we borrow their values.351
Simple signs with lines, circles, half circles or combinations are fairly common across352
many scripts and similarities increase with the number of signs available. To illustrate353
the defect in this method, we create a starter set for Latin based on some scripts that354
have signs common with the Latin alphabet as in Table 1 and it would be incorrect to355
use it to discover the values of the remaining Latin signs.356
2.5.3. Segmental, syllabic, logographic and word-fragment alphabet357
Some decipherments use arbitrary word fragments as sign values to fit as many in-358
scriptions as possible. This in itself is not necessarily incorrect as long as sufficient359
inscriptions can be read. Phonetic and syllabic decipherments would be expected to360
have some grammar but nearly all prior decipherment claims typically lack grammar,361
morphology, syntax, subject-verb agreement, plurals, conjugation, declension, preposi-362
tions or any other grammatical elements. Often there are supplementary logographic363
assignments to get around nonsensical readings. One way to justify the lack of grammar364
is to claim that all Indus inscriptions are names. Short inscriptions can plausibly be365
argued to be names since names outside sentences are essentially not declined. This366
also means most long inscriptions often won’t be readable.367

2.6. Solving a cryptogram379
Shannon’s method to solve a cipher:380
(1) Determine the language.381
(2) Determine the cipher (cryptogram for Indus script)382
(3) Determine one or more letters of the key by checking patterns of occurrence (fre-383
quency, variety of contact, doubles, reversals)384
(4) Use the deciphered key portion as a consistency check by testing other parts of385
the cryptogram.386
(5) Repeat the step above until fully deciphered.387
2.7. Determining the language388
The first step to solving a cryptogram is to guess the language itself. If the language389
is incorrect, the cryptogram will be unsolvable. Language is a Markov process and the390
probabilities of symbol transitions are very different even for related languages, which391
means that a large pattern set representing the corpus of one language cannot be read392
as another even if symbol values are reassigned. A cryptogram whose plaintext is in one393
language cannot be forcibly decrypted in another language due to the principle derived394
by Shannon:395
the amount of uncertainty we can introduce into the solution of the cryptogram cannot396
be greater than the key size ( Shannon, 1945).397

An analysis of Indus inscriptions also rules out Dravidian. Steve Bonta, a PhD in412

based on whether the language is head first or head final (Cinque, 1999). In Dravidian,424
the plural affix comes before the locative for example. Switching these around results in425
an illegal word. Also, these affixes by themselves (with rare exceptions) are not words426
and affixes cannot exist freely without a root or stem. The Indus inscriptions show427
the same strings of signs in initial medial and final positions, indicating that the Indus428
script cannot be agglutinative but is rather a fusional language.429

2.9. Determining one or more letters using occurrence patterns522
The next step is to guess or decipher the first sign using unusual patterns in the text523
that will enable us to read one or two syllabic words. We can rely on the fact that every524
seal is one or more complete words and choose the shortest available seals to avoid the525
issue of detecting word boundaries on longer inscriptions. With sufficient inscriptions526
that only have a single common undeciphered sign, we can uniquely determine its value.527
We can then repeat the steps and use the known signs to decipher the next unknown528
sign.529
All essential information for the cryptanalysis in a cryptogram is in the pattern of530
occurrences of the symbols and not the symbols themselves. The plaintext EEL may531
be enciphered as any of these equivalent cryptograms:BBA, XXC, GGF. Shannon calls532
such a pattern underlying a set of equivalent cryptograms a residue class:533
It is obvious in this case that these cryptograms are essentially equivalent. All that is of534
importance in a simple substitution with random key is the pattern of letter repetitions,535
the actual letters being dummy variables. Indeed we might dispense with them entirely536
indicating the pattern of repetitions ( Shannon, 1945).537
Cryptanalysis of cryptograms depends entirely on the pattern of positions and repeti-538
tions of signs in the ciphertext and does not depend on symbol shape, evolution history539
of a sign, assumptions on cultural aspects, geography, etc. Indeed, because none of540
these aspects are inputs, they can be credibly deduced as outputs once sufficient signs541
are deciphered.542
Patterns in the Indus corpus may be represented as regular expressions enabling543
us to search in dictionaries and determine their values. Solving cryptograms using544
regular expressions is a known art and we will only touch upon the techniques used for545
deciphering the Indus script (Goibhniu, 2007).546
2.10. The dictionary method of solving cryptograms547
We illustrate the dictionary method with a simple example of an unknown script en-548
coding text in the English language. There are 26 possible values for each symbol and549
trying each value would require 26! possibilities, which is infeasible. If we can, however,550
reduce the number of possibilities to solve an individual letter to two or three options,551
then the script can be deciphered in under a hundred attempts.552
2.10.1. Bootstrapping553
Let us suppose we parse through the corpus of the enciphered text and isolate the554
following words from the ciphertext. Whether these words were collected from one or555
many messages does not affect the decipherment as long as the values assigned to signs556
haven’t changed.557
Words with repeated signs have low equivocation and are great for bootstrapping558
our decipherment. An inscription pattern like /^(.)\1.$/ such as ǫǫλ matches the559
word EEL uniquely giving us {E, L }. Other words may decode different signs due to560
unique patterns such as P AP AY A and M ILLEN N IU M which can give a different561

Bootstrapping can be done even if there are no inscriptions with repeated signs, but563

2.10.2. Further progress565
Once we have a starting set of symbols, we can simply use them to find other sym-566

2.10.3. False negatives and false positives576
It is pretty clear from the method that the accuracy of the dictionary and the correctness577
of the regexes have a significant role in the correct decipherment of the script. Missing578
words may give a false negative and the sign cannot be resolved and it would appear579
we are at a dead end. At this point, we should check if the dictionary needs to be580
augmented from the possible candidates. Often not all conjugations, compounded stems581
or declined forms are available in dictionaries and false negatives should be expected.582
If a conjugated verb, plural noun or gerund form is obvious, then we may simply add583
it to the dictionary. If the sign being resolved is in the word stem and the other signs584
are all known, then it is sufficient to simply match the stem itself. For example, if the585
word KNOWING is missing in the dictionary, and we are trying to resolveKNO(.)ING,586
it’s sufficient to just match KNO(.). This is how long compound words in inscriptions587
can be matched.588
On the flip side, a false negative can lead to a false positive. Suppose in our example,589
the dictionary did not have the words LIE or LEE, the regex /^L.E$/' would match590
only LYE and ιwould be resolved to Y. This risk can be greatly mitigated by repeating591
the resolution of every sign using many different subsets of inscriptions, all of which592
should resolve to the same value. In addition, if there are plenty of attestations for593
the sign, grammatically correct and meaningful values for all of them reassure us that594
no false positive has been chosen. Incorrectly decoded signs will not match further595
plaintext words downstream and quickly result in a dead end making this approach596
self-correcting. For example, if ι is incorrectly assigned the value Y in our example,597
many plaintext words like ILL and IS will fail to match despite occasional plaintext598
words like DIE matching DYE. With sufficient distinct attestations, it becomes clear599
which signs are incorrect. Signs which have very few attestations may never resolve600
purely via dictionary check and a grammar check and a general theme consistency may601
be required.602

4. Discussion757
Indus script is a segmental script that may be described as proto-abugida. The major758
difference from Brahmi is that retroflexes use the same signs as dentals and aspirated759
and unaspirated stops use the same signs. Signs have a default vowel of अ a unless760
overridden by an immediately following vowel sign. No diacritics are used.761
Many signs have stylistic variations that have no phonetic distinctions. While signs762
are distinct in most cases, occasional ambiguities do exist, which are discussed below.763
Signs may be classed into several groups based on characteristics specific to them.764
4.1. The language765
Many words from classical literature such as the Ramayaṇa and Mahābhārata are at-766
tested, but the meanings of many archaic words are from the Vedic corpus. Chain767
compounds are also a feature of classical Sanskrit. Although the grammar is essentially768
the same, we refer to the Indus language as post-Vedic, to avoid confusion with the769
classical literature that began with Pāṇini’s Aṣṭādhyayī.770
4.2. The inscription content771
The contents of the Indus inscriptions are similar to Janapada coins and Gupta-era772
seals that they resemble( Azad, 2020). Long seals seem to use a concise Vedic con-773
cept as a motto or slogan. This tradition is attested at least from Gupta times and774
carries on to the present day. For example, a Gupta era seal readsśaṃkara-vara yānāb-775
hyaḥ(IndMuseum, 5th Century). This translates to “Shiva’s blessing for the journeys”776
which is a concise form of Rig-Veda 7.35.15. This could be a prayer for a journey of trade777
goods. Another reads Śivam brihaspate “[Our] welfare, O bṛhaspati”(MetMuseum, 6th778
CE). This is a concise form of Rig-Veda 1.89.6. The tradition of using Rigvedic for-779
mulas as prayers and mottos continues to the present day in many organizations. The780
government of India’s satyameva jayate “Truth alone triumphs” from the Chhāndogya781
Upanishad, is the most famous. In addition, the various state and local governments of782
India, Thailand and Indonesia continue to use concise forms of Vedic verses as mottos.783
The first few signs often invoke a deity. Most of these are of the meaning of destroyer784
or roarer. The Vedic god Rudra is both Roarer and Destroyer. This may be because the785
root ru means both roar and kill. Most names are simply constructed by the -a affix to786
the root, which creates the agent-noun from the verb-root (i.e., roarer from roar). In787

4.17. Script fidelity998
Bronze-age scripts generally do not have high fidelity to their transliteration. Linear999
B for example, has no consonant clusters. Sumerian is a polyvalent script that has1000
multiple ways to read and write a word and different signs are used based on meaning.1001
Even classical Arabic did not have dots (i’jām) to distinguish different phonemes on1002
many rock inscriptions, despite having multiple forms (initial, media, terminal) for the1003
same phoneme. By comparison, the Indus script has a fidelity similar to Tamil Brahmi1004
with nearly the same sign reuse groups of consonants. Indus inscriptions are fairly1005
readable even after 4-5 millennia with little effort.1006

10. Conclusion1165
The ability to read well beyond the unicity distance alone should be sufficient proof1166
of correct decipherment of the Indus script. This decipherment has many additional1167
compelling attributes. This is the only cryptanalytic decipherment and the only one1168
that uses well-established mathematical models and methods instead of guessing sign1169
values based on their appearance. This decipherment is the only full decipherment1170
and the only one where every sign and every stroke has been resolved, the only one1171
that is programmatically reproducible, the only one where the decipherment can be1172
followed sign-by-sign by the reader, the only decipherment that reads Semitic and mixed1173
inscriptions in addition to native IVC inscriptions, the only one that reads over 5001174
inscriptions including all 50 longest inscriptions grammatically correctly in an attested1175
language, the only one that validates research spanning almost a century from Hunter to1176
Heggarty. In addition, we have uncovered a remarkable number of additional evidence1177
such as reconstructed names of the signs, reasons for their allographs, and the clear1178
correspondence of derived sound values to known Brahmi values. We also show how1179
the constraints and habits of the Indus script carry on to Brahmi inscriptions of the1180
early historic era. Such a strong result is a first in any ancient script decipherment and1181
should be taken as plenary proof of decipherment of the Indus script.1182
